Who’s watching?

How Australians record their wildlife, and what the records leave out.

Every time someone notes a bird in a backyard, photographs a fungus or records a frog call, they create a wildlife record: a species, a place and a time.

Australia now holds 154 million records of its animals, plants and fungi. Nearly half come from volunteer birdwatchers, and six in ten are birds.

Kevin Kaung Myat Zaw | FIT3179 Data Visualisation 2 | Monash University | October 2026

Why wildlife records matter

Scientists cannot be everywhere. To learn where a species lives, whether it is spreading or disappearing, and how the seasons affect it, they rely on millions of individual records.

Those records are pooled by the Atlas of Living Australia and shared worldwide through the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF). They come from museums, herbaria and government surveys, and increasingly from the public through apps such as eBird, iNaturalist and FrogID.

This page asks what gets recorded, who records it, where and when people look, and why it matters. The national totals answer the first two questions. To answer the rest, the page follows one public project record by record.

Colours used on this page

1

What gets recorded

Some wildlife is easy to notice, name and love. The rest is small, hidden or hard to identify. The records show the difference clearly.

The national record has grown a hundredfold

In December 2007, GBIF held 2.6 million records about Australia. By October 2026 it held 259.5 million.

The steady climb comes from new apps, surveys and digitised museum collections. Recent jumps include large microbial DNA datasets, which is why the totals elsewhere on this page count only animals, plants and fungi.

How to read the chart. Each step is one quarterly GBIF snapshot of all records about Australia, including microbes. Hover anywhere to see the exact count for a snapshot.

Birds get a thousand times more attention than insects

Birds make up fewer than 1 in 100 of the 115,890 known species in these groups, yet they supply 63% of the records for these groups.

Insects are the opposite. They are more than half of all known species but only 4% of the records. Each bird species has about 112,000 records; each insect species has about 106.

Records follow people’s interests, not the variety of life itself.

How to read the chart. Each row is one group of wildlife. Purple bars to the left show its share of known species; teal bars to the right show its share of records. The dots on the far right show records per known species on a log scale, where each gridline is ten times the one before. Hover a row to highlight it in all three panels.

Who records Australia’s wildlife

Museums and government surveys built the early record. Today, volunteers with binoculars and phones supply a large share of it.

Nearly half of all records come from volunteer birdwatchers

The single biggest source is eBird, where birdwatchers log what they see. It supplies 56.4 million records about Australia. BirdLife Australia’s Birdata adds another 11.6 million.

Together these two volunteer programs supply 44 of every 100 wildlife records, including almost three-quarters of all bird records.

How to read the waffle. The grid has 100 squares, and each square is 1% of all 154 million records. Orange squares come from volunteer bird programs, grey from state government atlases, and pale squares from hundreds of other sources. Hover a colour to highlight it.

Spotlight: following one public project, record by record

National totals cannot show where volunteers go, when they go out, or how much each person contributes. For that, the rest of this page zooms in on one citizen-science project whose records are openly published in full: the Australian Museum’s FrogID.

Frogs are a good test case. Each species has its own call, so a phone recording is enough to identify it. FrogID now supplies more frog records to the Atlas of Living Australia than all other sources combined. In 2023, 12,434 people sent 84,321 recordings, and experts identified 136,621 frog records of 186 species in them.

The patterns that follow are typical of volunteer wildlife recording in general.

  1. 1Hear a frogUsually after dark, near water, in a garden, park or creek.
  2. 2Record it in the appThe phone attaches the time and place to the sound.
  3. 3Experts identify the callersMuseum frog-call experts name every species heard.
  4. 4The record is sharedChecked records go to the Atlas of Living Australia and into research.

A small group of dedicated volunteers does much of the work

A third of FrogID contributors, 4,435 people, sent a single recording. Together they supplied only 5% of the year’s recordings.

At the other end, just 90 people each sent more than 100 recordings. Their 22,166 recordings make up more than a quarter of the total.

How to read the waffles. Each grid has 100 squares. On the left, one square is 1% of the people who recorded; on the right, one square is 1% of the recordings. Darker orange means people who recorded more often. Hover a shade to find that group in both grids.

A national count week brings out the crowds

On an ordinary day before November, people sent about 237 recordings. During FrogID Week (3–12 November), the Museum’s national count, the daily average jumped six-fold.

In the eight days the published data covers, listeners sent 11,721 recordings, more than in any full month of 2023. Organised events like this, and the Aussie Bird Count for birds, are how volunteer science reaches its biggest numbers.

How to read the calendar. Each small square is one day of 2023: columns are weeks and rows are days of the week. Darker orange means more recordings that day. The outlined days are FrogID Week; grey days fall after the dataset ends.

Volunteers go out when the animals are active

Most frogs call at night, so most recordings are made in the evening. The busiest hour shifts through the year, from 5 pm in winter to 9 pm in summer, following sunset and daylight saving.

Every volunteer record reflects both the animal’s habits and the observer’s. That matters when scientists read the data.

How to read the clocks. Each clock is one season. Midnight is at the top and noon at the bottom; the day runs clockwise. A longer wedge means more of that season’s recordings were made in that hour, and the darkest wedge is the busiest hour.

3

Where people look

Volunteer records pile up where volunteers live. Following FrogID across the map shows how strongly that shapes what we know.

Records cluster around the cities

The square containing Sydney alone holds 13,040 frog records, from 1,607 people.

Frogs were recorded in only 353 of the roughly 770 one-degree squares that cover Australia. Large parts of the inland and the north have no records at all, which means no one recorded there, not that nothing lives there.

How to read the map. Each square is one degree of latitude by one degree of longitude. Darker teal means more records. Hover a square for its records, recordings, people and species.

Per person, the Northern Territory records the most

New South Wales produced 43% of all frog records from 31% of Australia’s population. Raw totals like this mostly show where people live.

Dividing by population gives a fairer comparison. On that measure the Northern Territory leads with 944 records per 100,000 residents, and South Australia is last with 224.

How to read the map. Darker teal means more frog records for every 100,000 residents. Hover a state to highlight it on both charts. On the right, a teal line means a state made a bigger share of the records than its share of the population.

The richest wildlife country has the fewest observers

Australia’s frogs are most varied in the far north-east. Around Cairns, listeners found 41 species in just 2,847 records.

Around Sydney it took 23,645 records, more than eight times as many, to reach the same 41 species. The places with the most to discover get the fewest visits.

How to read the map. Each purple circle summarises a 2° square (about 200 km across). A bigger circle means more species were recorded there. Hover a circle for its number of records and most-recorded frog.

4

When wildlife is recorded

Many animals are only easy to find in their breeding season. Because Australia spans the tropics and the cool south, that season depends on where you are.

North and south peak at opposite times of year

In the tropical north, more than half of a band’s yearly frog records can come in January and February, the height of the wet season.

In the south, records build through late winter and peak between August and October, after the winter rains.

How to read the heatmap. Each row is a band of latitude, with the north at the top. Each row adds up to 100%, so a dark cell means a large share of that band’s records came in that month. The bars on the right show how many records each band holds.

Each species has its own season

The Green Tree Frog stands out in January, the Common Eastern Froglet in winter, and Peron’s Tree Frog in late spring.

Because more people listen in some months than others, each species is compared with all frogs combined. When its colour rises above the dashed line, that frog was calling more than usual.

How to read the ridgeline. Each ridge is one species, and its height shows the share of that species’ records made in each month. The dashed line shows the same for all frogs, a guide to how much people were listening. Click a ridge to map that species below.

Watch a species switch on and off

The Green Tree Frog lights up the north and east in summer, then all but vanishes from the records by April. The Common Eastern Froglet is heard across the south-east all year but stands out most in winter.

Records kept year after year like these let scientists check whether species are breeding earlier as the climate warms.

How to use the map. Pick a species from the list (or click a ridge above), then drag the month slider or press Play the year. Grey dots show everywhere the frog was recorded in 2023; coloured dots show the chosen month.

5

Why every record counts

A single record proves that a species was alive in a particular place on a particular day. Rare species and spreading pests make that especially valuable.

Most species are rare in the data

Even in a project as busy as FrogID, 50 species were recorded fewer than ten times in 2023, and 13 only once. Most of them live in Western Australia, Queensland or the Northern Territory, far from the big cities.

For scientists, a single record from a remote place can be worth more than a thousand from a suburban pond.

How to read the dot plot. Each dot is one frog species. Species further right have more records, and every labelled step is ten times the one before. Dots in the shaded area have fewer than ten records. Click a family in the legend to show only its species.

Volunteers are also tracking invaders

The cane toad was brought to Queensland in 1935 and is still spreading. In 2023, 420 people recorded 1,048 cane toads through FrogID.

The westernmost record came from the West Kimberley in Western Australia, and toads were recorded down the New South Wales north coast. Records at the edges show how far the toad had reached, without anyone having to go looking for it.

How to read the map. Red dots are cane toad records; bigger dots mean more records in the same spot. Grey-green squares show where anyone recorded any frog: toads can only be found where someone is listening.

Look beyond the birds

Australians already record their wildlife on a huge scale. Volunteers supply a large share of the nation’s records and track pests, seasons and rare species.

But the records are lopsided. They favour birds over insects, cities over the inland, and weekends and count weeks over ordinary days.

The most useful record you can make is of something no one else is recording: an insect, a fungus, a frog in the far north. Apps such as iNaturalist, FrogID, eBird and Birdata send your sightings straight to the Atlas of Living Australia.

Notes on method

  • National figures cover animals, plants and fungi only; microbial records (bacteria, archaea and others) are excluded. Group definitions follow GBIF; species counts follow Chapman (2009), whose fish and insect figures are approximate.
  • GBIF counts every record available about Australia, whoever published it; counts change as datasets are updated.
  • About 42% of FrogID records stored their time in universal time (UTC); these were converted to local time. Six species names were matched to the official list.
  • Records show where and when people looked as well as where wildlife lives. Empty areas mean no records, not no wildlife.
