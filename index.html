Who’s watching?

How Australians record their wildlife, and what the records leave out.

Every time someone notes a bird in a backyard, photographs a fungus or records a frog call, they create a wildlife record: a species, a place and a time.

Australia now holds 154 million records of its animals, plants and fungi. Nearly half come from volunteer birdwatchers, and six in ten are birds.

Kevin Kaung Myat ZawFIT3179 Data Visualisation 2, Monash UniversityOctober 2026

Intro

Why wildlife records matter

Scientists cannot be everywhere. To learn where a species lives and how it is changing, they rely on millions of records.

A wildlife record is one species at one place and time. Records are pooled by the Atlas of Living Australia and shared worldwide through the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF).

They come from museums, herbaria and government surveys, and more and more from the public, through apps such as eBird, iNaturalist and FrogID.

Colours used on this page

  • Records: one species seen or heard at one place and time.
  • Species: the number of different kinds of plant, animal or fungus.
  • People: volunteers, their recordings and volunteer apps.
  • Government atlases: state wildlife databases.
  • Cane toad: an introduced pest (charcoal).

“Sightings” are records the Atlas classes as human observations: a person saw or heard the animal or plant, rather than collecting a museum specimen. They come from volunteers and from professional surveys.

1

What gets recorded

The national record has exploded in size, mostly through sightings logged in apps. But it is lopsided: some wildlife is easy to notice and name, and the rest is small, hidden or hard to identify.

The national record has grown a hundredfold

In December 2007, GBIF held 2.6 million records about Australia. By October 2026 it held 259.5 million.

Recent jumps include large microbial DNA datasets, which is why the totals elsewhere on this page count only animals, plants and fungi.

How to read the chart. Each step is one quarterly GBIF snapshot of all records about Australia, including microbes. Hover anywhere for the exact count.

Sightings rose 24‑fold since 1990

In 1990 the Atlas holds 0.55 million sightings. For 2024 it holds 13.1 million. Of all records dated 2025, 99% are sightings rather than museum specimens. Three volunteer apps, eBird, iNaturalist and Birdata, supplied about 85% of the sightings from 2016 to 2025.

Birds fill most of every year. The fastest growth is elsewhere: insect sightings rose from 13,769 in 2010 to 714,520 in 2024, and fungi from 6,782 to 152,087.

How to read the chart. Each coloured band is one group, stacked so the top edge is the total for that year; read the height of a band, not its edges. Click a group in the legend to fade the others.

Birds get a thousand times more attention than insects

Birds make up fewer than 1 in 100 of the 115,890 known species in these groups, yet they supply 63% of the records.

Insects are the opposite: more than half of all known species, only 4% of the records. Each bird species has about 112,000 records; each insect species has about 106.

How to read the chart. Each row is one group. Purple bars to the left show its share of known species; teal bars to the right show its share of records. The dots on the far right show records per known species on a log scale, where each gridline is ten times the one before. Hover a row to highlight it in all three panels.

2

Who records Australia’s wildlife

Museums and government surveys built the early record. Today, volunteers with binoculars and phones supply most of the new records, and each app has its own speciality.

Nearly half of all records come from volunteer birdwatchers

The single biggest source is eBird, where birdwatchers log what they see: 56.4 million records about Australia. BirdLife Australia’s Birdata adds 11.6 million.

Together these two volunteer programs supply 44 of every 100 wildlife records, including almost three-quarters of all bird records.

How to read the waffle. Each of the 100 squares is 1% of all 154 million records. Orange squares come from volunteer bird programs, grey from state government atlases, and pale squares from hundreds of other sources.

Each app has its speciality, and iNaturalist covers the small things

From 2016 to 2025, eBird supplied 80% of bird sightings. iNaturalist, where people photograph anything and the community identifies it, supplied 89% of insect sightings and 93% of fungus sightings.

Mammals are the exception: 65% of their sightings come through the NSW government’s BioNet Atlas.

How to read the heatmap. Each column is a wildlife group and adds up to 100%. Darker teal means a bigger share of that group’s sightings came from the source in that row; shares of 25% or more are printed. The strip on the left marks volunteer apps (orange) and government atlases (grey).

3

How volunteers record

National totals cannot show how people record. One open project, followed record by record, can.

Spotlight

Following one public project, record by record

National counts show what volunteers record. To see how they do it (how much each person contributes and when they go out), the next charts follow the Australian Museum’s FrogID, whose records are published in full.

In 2023, 12,434 people sent 84,321 recordings of frog calls, and experts identified 136,621 frog records of 186 species in them.

FrogID spotlight

A small group of dedicated volunteers does much of the work

A third of FrogID contributors, 4,435 people, sent a single recording, together only 5% of the year’s recordings. At the other end, 90 people each sent more than 100, more than a quarter of the total.

How to read the waffles. On the left, one square is 1% of the people who recorded; on the right, 1% of the recordings. Darker orange means people who recorded more often. Hover a shade to find that group in both grids.

FrogID spotlight

A national count week brings out the crowds

On an ordinary day before November, people sent about 237 recordings. During FrogID Week (3–12 November) the daily average jumped six‑fold: 11,721 recordings in eight days, more than in any full month of 2023.

How to read the calendar. Each small square is one day of 2023: columns are weeks and rows are days of the week. Darker orange means more recordings. The outlined days are FrogID Week; grey days fall after the dataset ends.

FrogID spotlight

Volunteers go out when the animals are active

Most frogs call at night, so most recordings are made in the evening. The busiest hour shifts from 5 pm in winter to 9 pm in summer, following sunset and daylight saving. Every volunteer record reflects the observer’s habits as well as the animal’s.

How to read the heatmap. Each row is a season and each column an hour of the day, in local time. Darker orange means a larger share of that season’s recordings. The outlined cell is each season’s busiest hour, with its percentage.

4

Where people look

Records pile up where people live and travel. That shapes what we know about every kind of wildlife.

Records crowd around the cities, for almost every group

The densest square, centred on Melbourne, holds 9.4 million records from 2016 to 2025: about 960 for every square kilometre. The Sydney and Brisbane squares follow.

Switch the group and the cities still dominate: for most groups the densest squares sit around Melbourne, Sydney and Brisbane, while the inland stays pale.

How to read the map. Pick a group from the list below the map. Each square’s colour shows its records per 1,000 km², and each class is ten times the one before. The classes stay fixed when you switch groups, so a rare group shows up paler overall. Hover a square for its exact numbers.

Per person, the Northern Territory records the most wildlife

Raw totals favour the big states. Dividing by population is fairer: each year the Northern Territory logs about 127,000 records per 100,000 residents, five times Western Australia’s 23,000.

The small, nature-rich territories and Tasmania punch above their weight, likely helped by visitors. New South Wales has 31% of Australians but 29% of the records.

How to read the map. Darker teal means more wildlife records each year for every 100,000 residents. Hover a state to highlight it on both charts. On the right, each lollipop shows a state’s share of records minus its share of residents, in percentage points; teal means more records than its population would suggest.

FrogID spotlight

The richest wildlife country has the fewest observers

Frogs are most varied in the far north-east. Around Cairns, FrogID listeners found 41 species in just 2,847 records. Around Sydney it took 23,645 records, more than eight times as many, to reach the same 41.

How to read the map. Each purple circle summarises a 2° square (about 200 km across); a bigger circle means more frog species were recorded there. Hover a circle for its records and most-recorded frog.

5

When wildlife is recorded

Records follow both the wildlife’s calendar and ours: breeding seasons, rain, holidays and count events.

Fungi in autumn, insects in summer, almost everything else in spring

Fungus records peak in May, when autumn rain brings out mushrooms: April to June holds almost half of the year’s fungus records. Insects peak in December and fall to about 3% a month in winter.

Birds, plants, reptiles and mammals all peak in October, the height of spring flowering and breeding. October is also the month of BirdLife Australia’s Aussie Backyard Bird Count. Frogs peak in November.

How to read the ridgeline. Each ridge is one group; its height shows the share of that group’s 2016–2025 records made in each month, and its peak is labelled. The dashed line shows all wildlife combined, so a ridge rising above it means that group is recorded more than usual that month.

The tropical north is recorded in the dry season, the south in spring

From 12°S to 16°S, July to October each hold up to 16% of the year’s records, while February to April hold as little as 3–5%. Travel is hardest in the wet season, and so is recording.

Further south, records climb from September and stay high through the summer holidays into January.

How to read the heatmap. Each row is a band of latitude, with the north at the top, and adds up to 100%. A dark cell means a large share of that band’s records came in that month. The bars show how many records each band holds. Click a month to show it on the map below.

Watch the recording season move across the map

In January, a typical northern square gets only a third of its usual monthly records. By July the same squares run at 1.5 times usual, while the south is quiet.

In October the pattern flips: southern squares run at about 1.4 times their usual month.

How to use the map. Drag the month slider, press Play the year, or click a month in the heatmap above. Teal squares are busier than their typical month, brown squares quieter, and pale squares about usual.

6

Why every record counts

A single record proves that a species was alive in a particular place on a particular day. For rare species and spreading pests, that proof is especially valuable.

FrogID spotlight

Most species are rare in the data

Even in a project as busy as FrogID, 50 species were recorded fewer than ten times in 2023, and 13 only once. Most live in Western Australia, Queensland or the Northern Territory, far from the big cities. A single record from a remote place can be worth more than a thousand from a suburban pond.

How to read the dot plot. Each dot is one frog species. Species further right have more records, and every labelled step is ten times the one before. Dots in the shaded area have fewer than ten records; the charcoal dot is the introduced cane toad.

FrogID spotlight

Volunteers are also tracking invaders

The cane toad was brought to Queensland in 1935 and is still spreading. In 2023, 420 people recorded 1,048 cane toads through FrogID, from the West Kimberley in Western Australia down to the New South Wales north coast. Records at the edges show how far the toad had reached, without anyone having to go looking for it.

How to read the map. Each charcoal dot is a 5 km square where a cane toad was recorded. Pale teal squares show where anyone recorded any frog: toads can only be found where someone is listening.

End

Look beyond the birds, and beyond the cities

The record is huge, growing fast and lopsided. Here is what it adds up to.

Australians now record their wildlife on a huge scale, and volunteer apps supply most of the new records. But the record is lopsided. It favours birds over insects, cities over the inland, spring over the wet season, and count weeks over ordinary days.

The most useful record you can make is of something no one else is recording: an insect, a fungus, a frog in the far north, a sighting in February. Apps such as iNaturalist, FrogID, eBird and Birdata send your sightings straight to the Atlas of Living Australia.

Notes on method

  • Atlas counts use the Atlas’s own species groups and its default data-quality profile. “Sightings” are records classed as human observations (volunteers and professional surveys). Records 2016–2025 are the latest ten complete years.
  • National figures cover animals, plants and fungi only; microbial records (bacteria, archaea and others) are excluded. Group definitions follow GBIF; species counts follow Chapman (2009), whose fish and insect figures are approximate.
  • GBIF counts every record available about Australia, whoever published it; counts change as datasets are updated.
  • About 42% of FrogID records stored their time in universal time (UTC); these were converted to local time. Six species names were matched to the official list.
  • Records show where and when people looked as well as where wildlife lives. Empty areas mean no records, not no wildlife.
  • Colours were chosen to stay distinguishable with colour-vision deficiency: red and green are never paired, and every chart has a legend or direct labels.
